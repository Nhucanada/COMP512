1. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP & METHODOLOGY

To evaluate the empirical behavior and scalability of our Paxos total order
consensus layer over the Group Communication Library (GCL), we conducted a
systematic parameter sweep using the automated stress testing harness
(TreasureIslandAppAuto via run_tiapp_auto.sh). 

The experimental matrix evaluated three independent variables:
  1. Process Group Size (N): 2, 3, and 5 concurrent player processes.
  2. Move Generation Interval (I): 100ms, 50ms, 20ms, 10ms, 5ms, and 0ms.
  3. Moves per Player: Fixed at 50 moves per player (yielding 100 total moves
     for N=2, 150 moves for N=3, and 250 moves for N=5).

All runs were executed with a fixed pseudo-random seed (randseed = 123456789)
to guarantee identical move sequences across trials. Testing was conducted on
McGill SOCS Unix servers (tr-open-xx). For every benchmark run, total execution
time (T_elapsed) was recorded from initial process launch until the final player
terminated. Consensus correctness was certified post-run using our automated log
auditor (verify_island_logs.py) to guarantee that all non-faulty nodes agreed on
the identical total order sequence without dropped turns.

Two primary performance metrics were extracted:
  • System Throughput: S = (Total Committed Moves) / (T_elapsed) [moves/second]
  • Average Move Latency: L = (T_elapsed) / (Total Committed Moves) * 1000 [ms/move]

In addition, individual player acceptance counts (M_i) were extracted from the
Island log (game-xx-99-*.log) to quantify fairness across processes under contention.

2. QUESTION A: IMPACT OF MOVE INTERVAL AND PLAYER COUNT ON LATENCY & THROUGHPUT
================================================================================

OBSERVATIONS (FIGURE 1 & FIGURE 2):
Referring to Figure 1 (System Throughput vs. Move Interval) and Figure 2 (Average
Move Latency vs. Move Interval):

1. Effect of Decreasing Interval:
   As the move interval is reduced from 100ms down to 10ms, system throughput
   grows steeply across all process group sizes. For N=3, throughput rises from
   28.52 moves/s at 100ms to 98.68 moves/s at 10ms (a 3.46x increase).
   Concurrently, average elapsed latency per move drops rapidly from 35.07ms down
   to 10.13ms. However, below 10ms (from 5ms to 0ms), throughput gains diminish
   sharply and plateau asymptotically.

2. Effect of Increasing Player Count (N):
   At large intervals (I = 100ms and 50ms), increasing the number of players
   produces higher aggregate throughput (at 100ms: N=2 achieves 19.31 moves/s,
   N=3 achieves 28.52 moves/s, and N=5 achieves 46.13 moves/s). Because players
   generate moves in parallel and the interval is much larger than consensus round
   duration, the system services interleaved requests without mutual interference.
   Conversely, as the interval approaches 0ms (high contention), the throughput
   advantage of adding more players disappears, and peak per-node throughput
   collapses.

DISCUSSION & THEORETICAL ANALYSIS:
Why does throughput behave this way?
At large intervals (100ms), system throughput is strictly throttled by the
application-level sleep timer (I). Because each consensus instance takes only
~2-4ms of network communication, the Paxos consensus layer spends >95% of its
time idle waiting for the next move generation. In this regime, aggregate
throughput scales linearly with N because each additional player contributes an
independent stream of moves into non-overlapping time slots.

As I approaches 0ms, the system transitions from "sleep-bound" to "consensus-bound".
At I = 0ms, players invoke broadcastTOMsg immediately upon completing their previous
turn. Multiple proposers concurrently initiate Phase 1 (PROPOSE) for the same
instance k. Under basic Paxos, concurrent proposers experience ballot conflicts:
if proposer P_j issues a higher ballot before proposer P_i completes Phase 2,
acceptors reject P_i's accept message or refuse P_i's proposal. P_i must then back
off, increment its ballot number, and re-execute Phase 1. 

Furthermore, as N increases, the majority quorum requirement grows (quorum = 2 for
N=2, 3, and quorum = 3 for N=5). Collecting promises and acceptances from a larger
quorum increases tail network latency, while the total GCL broadcast message volume
scales as O(N^2). Consequently, contention retries and serialization overhead cause
latency per move to increase with N under saturation.

3. QUESTION B: PROCESS FAIRNESS UNDER HIGH CONTENTION
================================================================================

OBSERVATIONS (FIGURE 3):
Referring to Figure 3 (Move Fairness Comparison at 100ms vs. 0ms):

1. Low Contention Regime (Interval = 100ms):
   At I = 100ms, moves are distributed with near-perfect equality among all
   participants. For N=3, each of the three players (A, B, C) captures exactly
   33.3% of the total accepted turns (50 moves each). For N=5, each of the five
   players captures exactly 20.0% of the total turns.

2. High Contention Regime (Interval = 0ms):
   As the move interval approaches 0ms, severe unfairness emerges. For N=3 at 0ms,
   Player A captures 49.3% of all accepted moves (74 out of 150), while Player B
   captures 27.3% (41 moves) and Player C captures only 23.3% (35 moves).
   For N=5 at 0ms, Player A dominates 33.6% of all turns (84 out of 250), whereas
   Player E wins only 12.8% (32 moves)—representing a >2.6x disparity.
   In all runs, Player A (the process with player number 1) consistently achieves
   the highest acceptance rate.

DISCUSSION & THEORETICAL ANALYSIS:
What algorithmic aspects cause this systematic asymmetry?

1. Ballot Number Tie-Breaking Rule:
   In Paxos, to guarantee that no two proposers issue identical ballots, ballot
   identifiers are constructed as monotonically increasing tuples:
       BallotID = (ballotSequenceNumber, processIdentifier)
   Total ordering dictates that (b1, p1) > (b2, p2) if b1 > b2, or if b1 == b2
   and p1 > p2. When multiple processes experience immediate contention at turn k,
   they frequently initiate proposals with the same local sequence number. Acceptors
   deterministically promise the higher BallotID and refuse or ignore lower BallotIDs.
   The process with lexicographical or numeric priority in processId wins Phase 1
   promises on the very first round, while lower-priority processes receive REFUSE
   replies and are forced into retry loops.

2. Local Loopback vs. Remote Network Latency:
   Every player process acts as both Proposer and Acceptor. When Player A broadcasts
   a PROPOSE message, its message reaches its own local Acceptor via zero-delay
   in-memory dispatch or local loopback. Reaching remote Acceptors requires TCP
   socket transmission across the GCL network stack. Because Player A's local
   acceptor is updated immediately, remote proposals arriving even 0.5ms later
   encounter an already-promised ballot and are refused.

3. GCL Socket Polling and FIFO Queue Ordering:
   The GCL implementation maintains an internal dispatch thread that polls point-to-
   point TCP socket channels in connection order. Earlier bound connections
   (typically process 1 / Player A) have their packets de-queued and handed to
   readGCMessage slightly ahead of higher-numbered processes, conferring a compounding
   first-mover advantage under saturation.

4. QUESTION C: MAXIMUM THROUGHPUT SATURATION (CEILING)
================================================================================

OBSERVATIONS (FIGURE 1):
1. Plateau Prior to Reaching 0ms:
   Examining the slope of the throughput curve in Figure 1, throughput does NOT
   continue to increase linearly as interval approaches 0ms. Instead, throughput
   effectively flattens out around Interval = 5ms to 2ms.
   For N=3, decreasing the interval from 10ms to 5ms increases throughput by 5.56%
   (98.68 to 104.17 moves/s). Further reducing the interval from 5ms to 0ms yields
   an incremental gain of only 2.1% (104.17 to 106.38 moves/s), while runtime
   decreases by less than 0.03 seconds.
   For N=5, throughput at 5ms (86.81 moves/s) is virtually identical to throughput
   at 0ms (87.72 moves/s)—a plateau difference of less than 1%.

2. Saturation Peak vs. Player Count:
   As the number of players increases, the maximum attainable throughput ceiling
   decreases. At I = 0ms:
     • N = 2 achieves a peak throughput of ~135.14 moves/sec.
     • N = 3 achieves a peak throughput of ~106.38 moves/sec.
     • N = 5 achieves a peak throughput of ~87.72 moves/sec.
   Adding more competing players degrades peak saturation throughput by 35%.

DISCUSSION & THEORETICAL ANALYSIS:
Why does the system plateau before reaching 0ms, and why does peak throughput drop with N?

1. Physical Consensus Latency Floor:
   Each Paxos instance requires at minimum two full sequential communication phases
   (Phase 1 Propose/Promise + Phase 2 Accept/Ack) plus external data serialization
   via ObjectOutputStream over TCP. On SOCS Unix network sockets, this protocol
   sequence has a physical lower bound of ~7-11ms per committed turn.
   Once the interval between moves drops below this consensus round-trip duration
   (around 5-10ms), the sleep interval is no longer the bottleneck. The application
   thread is already blocking in broadcastTOMsg waiting for majority acceptance.
   Decreasing the sleep interval to 0ms simply eliminates an idle gap that was
   already non-existent during active consensus.

2. Quadratic Message Complexity & TCP Socket Buffer Overhead:
   In GCL, broadcasting a message to N processes is translated into (N-1) point-to-point
   TCP socket transmissions. For each Paxos instance:
     • Phase 1: N broadcasts = N * (N - 1) messages
     • Phase 2: N broadcasts = N * (N - 1) messages
     • Phase 3: 1 commit broadcast = (N - 1) messages
   Total network messages per turn scale as O(N^2). For N=2, a turn requires only
   ~4-5 messages. For N=5, a turn generates ~44 messages across the local TCP mesh.
   At I = 0ms, TCP socket buffers and OS context switching between 5 JVM processes
   saturate the CPU, causing throughput to plateau at a lower ceiling for larger groups.

3. "No-Drop" Retry Cascades:
   Under the assignment's critical "No Dropped Moves" invariant, when Player B's
   proposal for turn k is preempted by Player A, Player B cannot drop its move.
   It must buffer the move and re-propose it for turn k+1. At I = 0ms with N=5, up
   to 4 players are simultaneously holding superseded moves and competing to push
   them into turn k+1. This creates a cascade of Phase 1 ballot preemptions (dueling
   proposers), consuming CPU cycles in abort/retry cycles that yield zero net progress
   until one proposer prevails.

5. SUMMARY & ARCHITECTURAL TAKEAWAYS

1. Low Contention (I >= 50ms): The system exhibits linear scalability with respect
   to player count and maintains perfect process fairness (equal turn distribution).
2. High Contention (I <= 5ms): The system hits a physical saturation plateau
   governed by Paxos 2-phase network round trips and TCP serialization latency.
   Reducing interval below 5ms yields diminishing returns.
3. Scalability Trade-off: While larger groups offer higher resilience (tolerating
   f = floor((N-1)/2) crashes), their peak saturation throughput decreases due to
   O(N^2) GCL message volume and larger majority quorum sizes.
4. Deterministic Fairness Bias: Under zero-interval saturation, ballot tie-breaking
   rules and local loopback timing confer a persistent advantage to lower-indexed
   processes (Player A), resulting in significant move distribution skew.
