1. EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY

Using the automated testing harness (TreasureIslandAppAuto via run_tiapp_auto.sh),
we benchmarked our Paxos total order layer across player counts N in {2, 3, 5} and
move intervals I in {100, 50, 20, 10, 5, 0} ms. Each run executed 50 moves per player
with a fixed seed (randseed = 123456789) on McGill SOCS tr-open-xx servers.
Correctness was verified using verify_island_logs.py to ensure identical total order
sequences across all players.

2. QUESTION A: IMPACT OF INTERVAL AND PLAYERS ON LATENCY & THROUGHPUT

Observations (Figure 1 & Figure 2):
As interval drops from 100ms to 20ms, system throughput grows dramatically: 3.75x for
N=2 (19.3 to 72.5 m/s) and 2.89x for N=3 (28.5 to 82.4 m/s). Concurrently, average move
latency drops from 51.8ms to 13.8ms (N=2) and 35.1ms to 12.1ms (N=3). However, below 10ms,
throughput gains taper off into an asymptote. Furthermore, while larger groups achieve
higher aggregate throughput at 100ms (46.1 m/s for N=5 vs. 19.3 m/s for N=2), this scaling
inverts under saturation at 0ms, where N=2 achieves 135.1 m/s while N=5 caps at 87.7 m/s.

Discussion:
At large intervals (I >= 50ms), the system is sleep-bound. Since an uncontended Paxos
round completes in ~2-4ms, the engine is idle >95% of the time. Parallel players submit
moves in disjoint time windows, scaling aggregate throughput linearly with N.
As interval approaches 0ms, the system transitions to consensus-bound contention. Proposers
concurrently broadcast Phase 1 proposals for the same turn, triggering ballot preemption
and retry loops. As N grows, the majority quorum requirement (floor(N/2) + 1) rises (from
2 to 3) and GCL broadcast message volume scales quadratically as O(N^2), increasing
tail network latency and reducing peak throughput for larger groups.

3. QUESTION B: PROCESS FAIRNESS UNDER HIGH CONTENTION

Observations (Figure 3):
At I = 100ms, turn distribution is perfectly balanced: exactly 33.3% per player for N=3,
and 20.0% per player for N=5. Under extreme contention (I = 0ms), severe disparity emerges.
For N=3 at 0ms, Player A captures 49.3% of all accepted moves, while Player B wins 27.3%
and Player C wins only 23.3%. For N=5 at 0ms, Player A captures 33.6% (84 moves) versus
only 12.8% (32 moves) for Player E—a 2.6x imbalance. In all runs, lower-numbered player IDs
consistently outperform higher-numbered players.

Discussion:
This systematic unfairness stems from three algorithmic and architectural factors:
1. Ballot Tie-Breaking Priority: Ballot numbers are tuples (ballotSeq, processId). When
   concurrent proposers initiate a turn with equal sequence numbers, acceptors deterministically
   promise the higher BallotID and refuse lower IDs. This priority advantage lets favored
   processes secure majorities on round 1, forcing others into backoff retries.
2. Local Loopback Latency: Each node acts as an Acceptor. A local proposal updates the local
   acceptor state instantaneously, while remote proposals must traverse the TCP stack. The
   local proposer holds an immediate 1-vote lead before remote packets arrive.
3. GCL Socket Polling Order: The GCL event loop polls TCP socket descriptors sequentially,
   dispatching packets from earlier-bound connections (Player A) slightly ahead of later
   connections, reinforcing a compounding first-mover advantage under saturation.

4. QUESTION C: MAXIMUM THROUGHPUT SATURATION (CEILING)

Observations (Figure 1):
Throughput does not scale continuously to 0ms. Instead, it flattens into a saturation
plateau between 5ms and 2ms. For N=3, dropping from 5ms to 0ms increases throughput by
only 2.1% (104.2 to 106.4 m/s). For N=5, throughput is virtually identical at 5ms
(86.8 m/s) and 0ms (87.7 m/s). Peak saturation throughput decreases with N: 135.1 m/s (N=2),
106.4 m/s (N=3), and 87.7 m/s (N=5).

Discussion:
The throughput ceiling is imposed by the physical round-trip floor of Paxos: two sequential
phases (Propose/Promise + Accept/Ack) plus Java Object serialization over TCP require
~7-10ms minimum per turn. Once move interval drops below this round-trip threshold, sleep
gaps disappear and threads block entirely inside broadcastTOMsg. Decreasing interval to 0ms
merely inflates internal contention queues without accelerating round completion.
Peak throughput drops as N increases because each broadcast translates to (N-1) TCP messages.
For N=5, a single turn generates up to 44 network packets. Socket buffer contention and JVM
context switching saturate the CPU, lowering the maximum achievable consensus rate.
Additionally, the "No-Drop" requirement forces superseded proposers to continuously re-propose
in subsequent turns, multiplying Phase 1 abort/retry traffic under saturation.

5. CONCLUSION

Paxos delivers high throughput and perfect fairness under moderate loads (I >= 50ms).
Under high contention (I -> 0ms), performance plateaus at a physical latency ceiling,
group scalability is constrained by O(N^2) message overhead, and deterministic ballot
tie-breaking produces severe process unfairness.
